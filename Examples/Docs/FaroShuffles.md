# Eight perfect shuffles

**Code:** [`FaroShuffles.swift`](../Sources/FunFacts/FaroShuffles.swift)

A **faro shuffle** cuts a deck exactly in half and interleaves the halves perfectly, card for card. Magicians and gamblers practice it for years, because it is not random at all.

There are two kinds. An **out-faro** keeps the top card on top. An **in-faro** tucks the top card to second place. For a standard 52-card deck:

- **8 out-faros** return the deck to its original order.
- **52 in-faros** are needed for the same thing.

## Why

A shuffle is a permutation, and repeating it walks every card around its cycle. The deck is back in order once every cycle has come around at the same time, which happens after the **least common multiple of the cycle lengths**. That number is the permutation's *order*.

The out-faro sends the card at position i (counting from 0) to position 2i mod 51, with the bottom card fixed, so its order is the smallest k with 2^k ≡ 1 (mod 51). Since 2^8 = 256 = 5 · 51 + 1, that k is 8. Counting positions from 1, the in-faro sends position p to 2p mod 53, and 2 has order 52 modulo the prime 53.

Persi Diaconis, Ron Graham and William Kantor worked out the group generated by both shuffles for every deck size. It's a classic paper connecting card tricks to finite group theory.

## In this package

`Permutation.outFaro(count: 52)` builds the shuffle, and `order` reads the cycle lengths off the Foata line and takes their least common multiple without performing a single shuffle. The example also checks that `raised(to: 8)` composes the out-faro back to the identity.

Then it does the shuffles for real. An `Arrangement` of the 52 cards in new-deck order applies `shuffle(.outFaro)` until `isInOriginalOrder` turns true, which it can check because an arrangement never moves its cards in memory, only the order it shows. It takes exactly 8 shuffles, as the cycle lengths predicted.
